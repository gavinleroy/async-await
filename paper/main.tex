\begin{abstract}
  Numerous modern programming languages include some form of
  asynchronous programming. In particular, a growing number now have
  what we call straight-line asychrony: attempts to provide
  asychronous functions that look similar to traditional functions,
  thereby enabling asychrony without introducing complex control.

  These languages often share construct names (like ``async'' and
  ``await''), which suggests that they have deep semantic
  similarities. Yet a close examination reveals that these languages
  are actually significantly different along numerous dimensions,
  often subtly; and that these differences have real semantic
  consequences. These kinds of differences tend to create confusion
  for developers moving between languages. They also make the space
  difficult to navigate for language designers who are either creating
  new languages or trying to add these features to existing ones.

  This paper therefore presents a \emph{design space exploration} of
  straight-line asychrony. We dissect several existing languages, and show how
  no two of them agree as a whole semantically. We create a taxonomy
  to explain their differences. We explore this space through concrete
  examples, prose discussion, and formal semantics. We also show how,
  as a consequence, languages can provide or preclude reasoning about
  certain program properties. Our ultimate goal is to help both
  programmers and language designers to deeply understand this
  important, rich, and subtle space.
\end{abstract}
